# Slide patterns

Read when building or reviewing one slide. Each entry gives the slide's job (the sentence the room leaves with), a
layout on the deck's grid, the figure that carries it, the usual miss and a worked example. The sources at the end
were read on 2026-10-26; the worked pitch's examples come from its October 2026 deck.

## The slide set, beat by beat

| beat | slide | takeaway | figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | what it is, in a line they can repeat | one quiet picture of the product's world |
| 2 | Why now | the change that makes it possible today | three or four dated tiles on one timeline |
| 3 | Problem | who hurts, how it's done today, what it costs | real artefacts side by side, a cost under each |
| 4 | Insight | what you know that others don't | a test the room plays, then the result |
| 5 | Product | the noun, the future it makes, how | in → inside → out, or three to five icon steps |
| 6 | Proof | it works, in one number | one chart with its conclusion written on it |
| 7 | Market | how big, bottom-up, and your share | waffle of $1M squares by who pays |
| 8 | Business model | who pays, for what, how much | a price list beside the market figure |
| 8 | Competition | the tools in use and the gap you fill | a check table, your row in the accent |
| 8 | Go-to-market | who buys first and how you reach them | a three-stage funnel with counts, or a timeline |
| 9 | Team | why these founders, with proof | logos with years, proof tiles that link |
| 10 | Ask | how much, for what, to which milestone | bars of line items, a milestone timeline |
| 11 | Close | the five-year picture and the ask | the opening picture, filled in |

Boards, not talk slides: financial projections, unit economics, risks and how you would know, IP and the regulatory
path, the methods behind each key number, the free alternative.

## Title: what it is

- **Job**: the company in one declarative line (Sequoia's "company purpose"; YC's template: the name and "a one line
  description of what you do").
- **Layout**: the name, the line, one quiet picture; twelve words or fewer.
- **Miss**: a tagline that needs the talk to decode it; a noun the room can't repeat ("platform").
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 01, "A shared map of every lab's control cells."

## Why now: the change

- **Job**: the shift in the world that makes this possible and urgent, with winners and losers (Raskin's first two
  steps; Sequoia: "why hasn't your solution been built before now?").
- **Layout**: three or four dated tiles left to right on one line, a number and a source in each, the latest in the
  accent. Say it once, briefly: investors spent 27 seconds on why now and why you in successful decks, 62 in failed
  ones (DocSend data, TechCrunch 2019).
- **Miss**: a generic trend ("AI is growing"); why now repeated on three slides.
- **Example**: a pitch deck's strip: 2023, 1.8B public cells; 2025, FDA: fewer animal tests; 2025, $30M per cell map;
  now, AI reads cells.

## Problem: today, and what it costs

- **Job**: one person's pain in the room's terms (Sequoia: "Describe the pain of your customer"; YC: how it impacts
  real people).
- **Layout**: the person's question as the headline; two or three ways it's done today side by side, specific enough to
  recognise (the query as typed, the email to a paper's authors), the cost under each in money or months.
- **Miss**: a market statistic standing in for a person; four slides proving the problem.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 02b–02c, "Do my cells look 'normal'?", then search, ask, guess: "$100,000s", "months".

## Insight: the test, then the claim

- **Job**: what you know about the problem that everyone else doesn't, specific and plain (Seibel).
- **Layout**: a game the room can play first, then the answer view with the result filling the zone and chance marked.
- **Figure**: a confusion matrix with the diagonal lit and chance labelled; a scatter with ρ in a corner.
- **Miss**: an insight that is really a feature; the claim before the evidence.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 03a–03b: six images, one lab or six?; then an 11 × 11 matrix where a model names the lab from
  control cells alone, chance 9%.

## Product: the noun, then the promised land

- **Job**: what it is in one sentence, then what life looks like with it, then the few things it does to get there
  (Raskin: the promised land is "a new future state, not your product").
- **Layout**: in → inside → out, left to right (who gives what, free; what sits inside; who pays, for what); or three to
  five steps as icons with a word each. A list of steps beats a screenshot, which is small, busy and slow (Hale).
- **Per audience**: first one line on why everyone wants it (the question they all ask, the one thing that answers it:
  "Every lab asks 'do my cells look normal?' One shared map answers everyone."), then one tile each, the gain in six
  words or fewer and one number the talk already showed.
- **Miss**: a demo before the noun; a screenshot; one slogan per audience.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, "One shared repository of raw cell images": labs give control images free, the map aligns
  them, subscribers get a certificate and an API. "Scientists: catch a drifting batch before the screen. 3 batches
  flagged from controls alone."

## Proof: traction, or the best evidence

- **Job**: it works, in one number (YC's template: "Make the numbers clear and meaningful"; Seibel: what you have done
  for the time you have spent).
- **Layout**: one chart filling the zone with its conclusion written on it (Hale: "CliffsNotes right on the slide"),
  the key number top right.
- **Before revenue**: a result on real data, a pilot, a letter of intent, usage; the honest paid count, even zero.
- **Miss**: a chart without a unit; a cumulative curve that hides a stall.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 04c and 05: control drift tracks weaker drug signal (ρ −0.707 over 118 batches); one drug,
  one shared change, nuclear area ×2.1 across 11 labs.

## Market: bottom-up

- **Job**: how big if every likely buyer paid, and the share you aim for. Count customers and multiply by price;
  bottom-up avoids a customer drawn too wide (Seibel).
- **Layout**: the total in the headline; a figure split by who pays, each stream's count × price on stage; the target
  outlined in the accent, and the path to it over time beside it (revenue by year to the target, its growth rate named and
  marked as an assumption). Every rate written one way ($10k/line/quarter, $150k/year).
- **Miss**: top-down ("1% of China", Kawasaki's example of a bad pitch); one assumed price times a few counts as bars;
  a target with no path to it; "a year", "per" and "/" mixed on one slide.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 07, "Every likely buyer: $84M/year. / Five-year target: a tenth.": 84 squares of $1M
  (checks $64M, certificates $15M, licences $3.75M, benchmark $1M), the $8.4M tenth outlined, and under it an assumed
  path, first revenue in year 2 growing about 4x a year to the tenth in year 5 (2026-10-26: "show exponential
  revenue growth over time. use per or /").

## Business model: who pays for what

- **Job**: one simple model (Seibel: "Own the simple business model").
- **Layout**: a price list as the market figure's legend: who pays, for what, at what price, how many; the free side
  named; what is not priced yet, said plainly.
- **Miss**: a potpourri of models; prices without counts.
- **Example**: a pitch deck: academic labs free; companies pay for checks and certificates; AI teams by API.

## Competition: the field's everyday tools, fairly

- **Job**: what people use today, what each covers, the gap only you fill (Sequoia: "Show that you have a plan to win").
- **Layout**: a check table, the five or six tools the field names daily as rows (each with its source), the three or
  four steps of the work as columns, your row last in the accent filling the shared blind spot; your own gaps said
  plainly.
- **Figure**: a check table; a 2×2 only when both axes are real, measurable things.
- **Miss**: self-serving axes; logos without what they do; leaving out the free alternative.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 08, "Each tool sees one lab. / a pitch deck sees every lab.": CellProfiler, Harmony,
  pycytominer, Genedata Screener, JUMP and Recursion against measure cells, normalise plates, reference data, every lab
  over time.

## Go-to-market: how the first customers come

- **Job**: who buys first, how you reach them, what one costs to win (Kawasaki lists marketing and sales among his ten).
- **Layout**: the first segment by name, the channel, the next two steps, as a short funnel with counts or a timeline.
- **Miss**: "viral" or "partnerships" with no names and no numbers.
- **Example**: none yet in a pitch deck; the three partner labs in the raise are the seed of one.

## Team: why us

- **Job**: the founders' fit to this problem, with proof (Seibel: an "extremely relevant credential"; YC's template:
  "Nobody cares about your advisors").
- **Layout**: institutions as logos with years and one skill each, work and education labelled apart; three or four
  record tiles, each linking its proof (the publication list, the dataset paper, the patent applications by title and
  number); the full paper list in the reader's layer; the hire you need named.
- **Miss**: a CV; advisors first; a role bigger than the founder's.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 02a, the founder first; the leader the company needs named on the close ("looking for").

## Ask: how much, for what, to where

- **Job**: the amount, what it buys line by line, and where it gets you (YC's template: "how much money you need, and
  what it gets you").
- **Layout**: the amount and runway in the headline; horizontal bars of every line item with its dollar figure; three
  or four milestones on a timeline, the one that triggers the next round in the accent.
- **Miss**: a round number with a pie of "R&D, sales, ops"; no milestone.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 09, pre-seed for 18 months to first revenue: a leader $360k, a playground of about 60 plates
  $240k, 3 partner labs $60k; milestone 4, "first paid certificate", leads to the seed round.

## Close: the vision and the ask

- **Job**: what you will have built in five years (Sequoia's "Vision"), calling back to the opening, the ask and a
  contact on screen (Kawasaki: "Summary and call to action").
- **Layout**: the opening picture, filled in; one line.
- **Example**: a pitch deck, 10, a grid of batches with an open row: "129 batches today; the open row, yours."

## Figures by message

| message | form | accent |
|---|---|---|
| a change over time | a line with one threshold | the point that crosses |
| counts, money, parts of a whole | a waffle of rounded squares, one unit each | the target or your share, outlined |
| activity or coverage | a GitHub-style grid of rounded squares in four shades | the open or newest row |
| a few items compared | sorted horizontal bars, labels on the bars | your bar |
| two measures related | a scatter, ρ in a corner | the one point |
| a classifier works | a confusion matrix, chance marked | the diagonal or your row |
| before and after | paired images or a slope chart on one scale | the after |
| where each sits among many | a map with rings for spread | your dot |
| a process | three to five icon steps, a word under each | the step you add |
| tools against steps | a check table | your row |
| a plan | a timeline of three to five milestones | the one that unlocks the next round |
| cells or tissue | two-channel crops, one cell centred, scale bar, channel key bottom left | the treated cell |

## Patterns from decks that raised

Read in primary sources on 25 Oct 2026 and re-checked by a second agent: SEC filings (Ginkgo's 2021 investor
presentation, Recursion's S-1 and 2025 earnings deck, Snowflake's S-1), company pages and one teardown. These are
late-stage public materials; seed decks were not public, so their lessons come from the teardown and YC's guidance.

- **Borrow a shape the room knows, then say what is inside it**: "the GitHub of machine learning" (Hugging Face, press
  2022), "R&D Cloud" (Benchling), "Data Cloud" (Snowflake S-1), "Recursion OS". One plain shape, kept on every slide.
- **Name the data asset, with a count that grows**: "Codebase, Ginkgo's scale data asset" (2021); "nearly eight
  petabytes, growing about 80 TB a week" (Recursion S-1, 2021); "2M+ models, 500k+ datasets" (Hugging Face).
- **Two sides, one line each**: a free side that builds the asset, a paid side that funds it (Benchling: free for
  academics, paid tiers for industry; Ginkgo: usage fees now, value share later).
- **One value phrase per audience, in its words** (Owkin: patients, institutions, researchers, data scientists).
- **Who pays, as names and numbers**: Recursion's "$150M upfront" from Roche; a teardown of a seed deck faulted
  "Revenue? Great. How much, though?!" (TechCrunch on Pepper Bio, 2024).
- **Leadership as a measurable future in the field's own word**, with evidence you are on the way (Recursion: "secure
  its future as the TechBio leader"; Benchling: one in four biotech IPOs built on it).
- **Pre-empt the free alternative**: when a public archive gives the data away (the Cell Painting Gallery: 656 TB,
  CC0), say on the slide what the product adds (live controls from every lab, aligned, with metadata, a certificate,
  an API).

## Sources (read 2026-10-26)

- Sequoia Capital, "Writing a business plan" (sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan): company purpose,
  problem, solution, why now, market potential, competition, business model, team, financials, vision.
- Aaron Harris, "How to build your seed round pitch deck", YC Library (Winter 2018 batch): title, problem, solution,
  traction, insight, business model, market, team, ask; each slide the first of a set of at most three.
- Michael Seibel, "How to pitch your company", YC Library: seven questions (what you do, market, progress, unique
  insight, business model, team, the ask), the email test, no jargon or "platform".
- Kevin Hale, "How to design a better pitch deck", YC Library: legible, simple, obvious; five to seven ideas; text at
  the top; write the conclusion on the chart; no screenshots; animations and transitions listed among distractions.
- Russ Heddleston (DocSend), "Pitching your product will kill your fundraising", TechCrunch, 12 Apr 2019: decks that
  raised opened on purpose, then why now and why you; failed decks drew four times the time on product slides; about
  18 pages and 3.7 minutes a deck.
- Guy Kawasaki, "The 10/20/30 rule of PowerPoint", 30 Dec 2005: ten slides, twenty minutes, nothing under 30 points
  (or the oldest listener's age halved), because the room reads ahead of the speaker.
- Andy Raskin, "The greatest sales deck I've ever seen", Medium, 15 Sep 2016: name a big change, show winners and
  losers, tease the promised land, features as gifts that overcome obstacles, evidence.
- Seth Godin, "Really bad PowerPoint", 29 Jan 2007: no more than six words a slide; slides reinforce the voice.
- Duarte, "The Glance Test": the meaning of a slide in three seconds or less.
- Aurora Harley, "Icon usability", Nielsen Norman Group, 27 Jul 2014: few universal icons, labels visible at all
  times, one meaning per icon, the five-second rule.
- Rougier, Droettboom and Bourne, "Ten simple rules for better figures", PLOS Computational Biology 10(9): e1003833,
  2014.
- Claus O. Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*, ch. 24, "Use larger axis labels": check figures scaled down and
  from across a room.

## A biotech services deck (Axiom Bio, 2025)

From a biotech services deck that reads well at a glance (Axiom Bio, "Axiom Intro", 2025, 30 slides):
- **"Bold topic:" plus one claim** as every title, in one fixed spot ("Bioactive point of departure: Axiom measures
  changes ... much lower than traditional toxicity readouts").
- **One accent means "us"**; grey is the old way, red is failed or withdrawn, green is marketed or safe, on every slide.
- **Them vs us as paired cards**: a published benchmark from a named leader beside your result on the same held-out
  set, with a cost bar under each ("CRO $3k–$5k" grey, "$100–$450" in the accent).
- **The brand mark as a diagram node**: the logo's object stands for the product inside flows and on the key point of
  a figure, so the eye finds "us" in every diagram.
- **Pipeline rows left to right**: input icon, real images, readout table, outcome silhouette, one row per service
  with a coloured label tab.
- **Evidence grids for images**: control row over treated row, column chips per readout, arrows on the phenotype,
  numbered zoom insets, legend words coloured by their channel.
- **Stat tiles and motif tiles**: a big number with a two-word label; a structure or icon with its accuracy and a
  one-line metric.
- **Cases from real headlines**: dated news cards beside the model's call ("could have saved tens of millions"), a
  failed and a marketed drug as a red/green pair, each suggested fix with its effect ("reduces toxicity by 52%").
- **A gradient band names zones on a dose strip** (non-toxic, bioactive, toxic) above the images at each dose.
- **One quiet frame**: white rounded cards on warm off-white, small page numbers, the same hero on the first and
  last slide with the URL.
