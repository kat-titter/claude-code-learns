# Storytelling

People follow cause and consequence, a question they want answered, and something at stake far more readily than a
list of facts, and remember it longer (Dahlstrom 2014). This skill owns the arc, the opening and the close; the medium
skills apply them.

## Gotchas

- Sections in the order they were written rather than the order a reader reasons ("I still don't understand how this connects"): reorder by cause and consequence (§2).
- An ending that summarizes: close on a callback or the next step (§5).
- The writer's journey up front ("less focus on me/my"): open on the reader's problem (§3).

## 1. Find the one sentence

- **Say the whole piece as And, But, Therefore** (Olson 2015): what we know, *and*; the problem or surprise, *but*;
  what follows, *therefore*. a pitch deck: "Every result is a difference from a control, and labs check controls
  locally, but a plate can pass local QC and still look like another lab's, therefore put every lab's controls on one
  map." No *but* means no story yet; three *ands* before it means the opening starts too early.

- **Rounds of notes erode the spine; when the verdict turns global, rebuild the outline.** Each round of local fixes
  (a figure here, a cut there) leaves the order the page grew in. When a reader says "long, confusing, no movement"
  while the content is all there, stop patching: measure words and figures per section, say the piece as And, But,
  Therefore, and rebuild the order from that sentence before touching another element (a peptide explainer, 2026-11-06:
  "all the content is there but doc is long, confusing, lacks clarity, structure, and movement through the piece",
  after five builds, a splice and six review rounds; the market story sat in three places, 38% of the words).

## 2. Reason the way readers do

- **Concrete, then abstract.** An event, an object or a number the reader can picture, then the principle; name a
  thing after the reader has met it.
- **Given, then new** (Clark & Haviland 1977). Start each sentence from what the reader already holds and end on the
  new, important part, where the stress falls (Gopen & Swan 1990). The same holds for marks: a code, glyph or colour
  scale appears only after the page has explained it. An opening figure uses only what a newcomer can read cold
  (a peptide explainer hook, 2026-11-17: green proof squares sat above the chapter that explains them, "I don't know what
  these green squares mean ... we're starting from scratch").
- **State the main point, then check the chain arrives at it.** Say the point plainly where the reader looks first (a
  headline, a lead sentence), then walk the steps under it and confirm each one leads to the next and the last one lands
  on the point. A missing middle step reads as a leap ("where a lab sits predicts what it measures" needed "controls
  drift, and in the same batches drug answers move; the controls alone flagged them"). Say what you mean: the claim
  itself in plain words ("the rewrite left every file as it was"), not a gesture at it ("the results look good").
  (2026-10-25: "always make main point clearly, check chain of reasoning is clear and complete (arrives at point)";
  2026-10-26: "always say what you mean... maximize Truth".)
- **One chain of because.** Each step follows from the last ("so", "but", "which means"); a step that does not
  connect moves or goes. Order every element the same way: a figure, fold or term comes after the text that explains
  it, and each section opens on the idea the last one closed with (an essay: chapter 10 ends on time's counter,
  11 opens on "a count that never comes back"; 2026-10-25: "i still dont understand how this connects?").
- **Show the reasoning with the verdict**: the evidence a reader can check, how sure you are, the strongest objection
  and why it does not hold, and what would change your mind. People judge arguments better than they produce them (Mercier & Sperber
  2017), so give them one worth judging.
- **One question the reader carries** from the opening to the close; detours are fine while it stays open. Each
  section opens on, or turns on, a smaller question of the reader's own that leads into it ("Can a peptide skip the
  needle?"), and the big question comes back near the end ("So how would you know?"); a page with one question in
  its whole main read reads as a lecture (a peptide explainer, 2026-11-17: "more leading questions, humanize text";
  1.6 to 8.9 questions per 100 sentences).
- **Group sections by one axis, the subject; a shared rating is a column, not a chapter.** Two things that share
  a chapter only because both score low on the same measure belong apart, each in its own chapter, with the rating
  shown once in the table that ranks everything (and chapter order may follow it). a peptide explainer, 2026-11-17: "why
  BPC157 and motc together? maybe they should be separate?" They had shared a chapter for thin human proof, though
  one is a stomach fragment tested in rats and the other a mitochondrial hormone, so they were split and the chapters
  now run in descending order of proof.
- **In a methods or process section, each key choice carries its reason, tied to the thesis.** Name what was chosen,
  then one "why" line that points back to the main claim, so the process argues instead of listing steps (a generative
  artwork's methods page became "every step a choice": style, taste, grammar, slow walk, one laptop, each with a why
  back to "the whole difference is a choice"; 2026-10-26: "highlight key choices, explain why... connect
  everything back to thesis").

- **Organize top down and bottom up before writing.** List what connects, what goes deeper and what each deep part
  needs first; order it background, the gap it leaves, a few examples, then depth on one example (a comparison of real
  products, say), and link related facts visually with one colour per thing, linked highlights and hover cards. (Peptide
  docs, 2026-11-05: "what background info is required before going deeper? ... loop, background and gap identification,
  examples, depth on one example (ex copper multiple commercial comparison)".)

## 3. Open

Earn the second sentence with the first. Pick the opening that fits the reader:
- **A concrete moment**: Quanta opens feedback control on a robot being shoved; Distill's cellular automata on a lizard
  regrowing; a lesson skill on one remark ("17 s a frame seems like a lot").
- **The question the reader already asks**: "Why does my agent keep making the same mistake?"
- **A gap**: one fact that does not fit, so curiosity pulls the reader forward (Loewenstein 1994).
- **The payoff, for readers who scan** (READMEs, reports, emails): the answer in one line ("Correct it once. It
  remembers."), then the story.
- **The why, right after the hook**: the reason the subject exists comes before its machinery, so every later
  section answers a question the reader already holds (an essay, 2026-10-26: "why a cell carries a copy
  of its world ... should come at beginning"; it had sat in chapter 06, after the table it explains).
Drafts often begin a paragraph late; try deleting the first one.

## 4. Hold attention

- **Voice holds attention too**: presence, specifics, candor and timing live in prose-voice.

- **Stakes in the reader's terms**: who gains or loses, and how much.
- **Tension, then the turn**: the problem before the solution; the honest failure with its before and after.
- **Surprise, then the explanation.** Heath & Heath's sticky ideas are simple, unexpected, concrete, credible,
  emotional and told as stories (2007).
- **One governing image** for the whole piece, one small analogy per hard idea.
- **A lead paragraph is the build before the drop**, like a DJ track: context, the pull between pro and con, a
  question left open, then the figure is the payoff, so the text moves the reader into it. Show the page's structure
  rather than describe it ("each name under the drawing opens the chapter" is a description). A section title names the experience
  ("Peptides acting in the cell, a tour"), not the content type (a peptide explainer, 2026-11-06: "robotic writing ...
  storytelling with text: context, pro/con, suspense, payoff, like a DJ song! The text should move you into the figure").
- **Rhythm**: a short sentence after a long one lands the point. A little dry humour where the point lands is welcome.

## 5. Close

People remember the peak and the end (Kahneman et al. 1993), so the close carries weight. Pick one:
- **Answer the opening question** plainly, with the number.
- **Call back** to the opening image, changed by what came between.
- **The so-what and one next step**: what becomes possible, and one specific thing to do (an install line, an ask,
  a link).
End on the strongest line and stop; summaries serve scanners best at the top.

## 6. Check it is engaging and clear

- Read only the first and last sentences: do they make a promise and keep it?
- Read only each paragraph's first sentence: does the story still hold (a reverse outline)?
- Tell it aloud in 30 seconds to someone outside the field; wherever they ask "why?", the chain has a gap.
- Each term the reader meets is met before it is named, or cut.

  one under the reader's hand, and say in the lead that this is the point. Case: an essay's chapter 11, both tables
  dragged from grid to spiral (2026-10-25: "this periodic table is actually loop very important reveal true geometry.
  thats the whole punchline").

- **A product page or tutorial tells one real case, demos the live product inline, and closes on that case.** Open on
