# Prose voice

Clear prose gets read; prose with a person in it gets remembered and forwarded. Voice comes from choices a reader can
feel: what you notice, what you admit, where you linger, and when you let a sentence smile. Clarity first
(humanize-science-writing), the arc next (storytelling), then this.

## Gotchas

- A joke added after the draft reads as forced: humour comes from a true detail and its timing (§4).
- Warmth that drifts into "me" and "my": turn it toward the reader and others' work (§5).

## 1. Someone at the desk

- **A person with a view.** Say what you think, how sure you are, and what changed your mind; an opinion held lightly
  reads warmer than a survey of positions.
- **A little of the life around the work**: the night the render crashed, the coffee that went cold, the friend whose
  question started it. One such detail per section is plenty.
- **Why it matters to you, said once and plainly.** Delight counts as a reason ("this is the part I love").

## 2. Specific beats general

- **The exact noun**: "a 40 ms flam", "a stainless utility sink", "384 wells imaged in one night", not "a timing
  issue", "a fixture", "lots of data".
- **Sensory where it fits**: what it looked, sounded or felt like, in one concrete image, then move on.
- **One image per idea**, and the image does work: it explains as well as decorates.

## 3. Candor

- **Say the true, slightly embarrassing thing**: the dumb first try, the bug that took a week, the result that
  disappointed. Candor earns trust, and it is often the funniest line in the piece.
- **Name the limits in your own voice**, as a person would to a colleague, not as a disclaimer.

## 4. Humour from truth and timing

- **Funny comes from the exact word and the true observation**: understatement, a deadpan aside, a callback, a
  comparison nobody expected but everyone recognizes ("a goldfish in a lab coat").
- **Timing**: the turn goes last in the sentence, the short sentence after the long one, the pause of a line break.
- **Aim it at yourself or the situation**, with the reader in on it; let the joke stand without explaining it; one laugh a
  section beats a routine.

## 5. Warmth and spirit

- **Talk to one reader, as a friend in the room**: "you" where it helps, questions they would ask, credit to the
  people and work you build on, said generously and by name.
- **Energy lives in verbs and tense**: verbs that move, present tense for scenes, a sentence that speeds up when the
  idea does.
- **Let real excitement show** where it is real; it is contagious when earned and grating when sprayed.

## 6. Beauty at the sentence level

- **Sound**: read it aloud; vary length so the rhythm breathes; end sentences and paragraphs on the strongest word.
- **Natural register**: contractions, plain words, the way you would say it, then tidied. Formal only where the venue
  asks.
- **Leave room for wonder**: one line per piece can step back and look at the thing with fresh eyes.

## 7. Bring a flat draft to life

1. **Mark the three flattest sentences** (abstract, hedged, or replaceable by any writer).
2. **Replace each** with a specific, a candor or a turn from the sections above.
3. **Cut what dulls**: stacked hedges, explained jokes, tidy morals, symmetrical triplets, the AI tells in
   humanize-science-writing section 5.
4. **Read aloud once**; fix every place you stumble or wince.
5. **Check**: is there one line someone would quote, one sentence only this writer could write, and would a friend
   forward it?

## Voice models: good human writing

Model voice on writing people love that no machine drafted. A person's own voice comes from their best unassisted writing:
posts and essays first, then casual email. Their application letters, statements and form writing are poor voice
models, even their own (2026-10-26: "use non-ai generated, good writing examples. SOP probably
not good"). Read a page of the nearest model before drafting, and take the one quality it teaches:
- **Wonder in biology**: Lewis Thomas, *The Lives of a Cell*; Ed Yong, *An Immense World*. Short essays that make a
  cell or a sense feel astonishing without inflating a claim.
- **Warmth and candor**: Oliver Sacks's late essays (*Gratitude*); Anne Lamott, *Bird by Bird*. A person plainly at
  the desk, admitting things.
- **Funny science**: Mary Roach (*Stiff*, *Gulp*); Randall Munroe, *What If?*. Exact specifics and deadpan timing.
- **Clear with personality**: Paul Graham's essays; Richard Feynman, *Surely You're Joking*; Bartosz Ciechanowski's
  explainers. Spoken plainness, one idea at a time.
- **Gorgeous sentences**: Annie Dillard, *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*; Joan Didion's essays. Rhythm and the exact noun.

The list and its descriptions are from memory and unverified; quote at most a short line from any of them.
